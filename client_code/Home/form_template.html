Activity 6A: Georgia Candy WoZ

Background

In a Wizard of Oz study, a person performs work that a user might otherwise expect a computer to do. Here, both partners know that a human is playing the system. The conversation helps us investigate the language a future system would need to handle.

An intent label is a category people assign to an utterance, such as asking a price or making a recommendation. The class will annotate its conversations and train one Naive Bayes classifier together. The categories and examples we choose shape what that classifier can recognize.

Learning objectives

  • Experience collecting dialogue through a Wizard of Oz interaction.
  • Discuss and justify intent annotations for both customer and worker turns.
  • Explain how category choices, context, and training examples affect intent recognition and the user experience.

Instructions

  1. Pair up. Choose a Human (customer) and a Wizard (candy store worker). Each person enters a name and selects their role below.
  2. Meet in a room. The Wizard creates a room and shares its code. The Human joins with that code and reads a randomly assigned customer role, visible only to the Human.
  3. Talk for about 10 minutes. The Human uses the role as a starting point and speaks naturally. The Wizard uses the catalogue to respond. Ask questions, explore choices, or change your mind; do not follow a script. Start a new dialogue when one exchange is finished. Use +2 minutes if you need more time.
  4. Annotate together on one computer. When time is up, use the Wizard's computer to label each turn. Choose one best-fitting intent per turn, including worker-side intents. Discuss ambiguous cases and note what a single label leaves out.
  5. Reflect as a class. After saving annotations, return here and discuss the questions below while other pairs finish. Your instructor will train and test one classifier using the class data.

This is a fictional shop: use made-up customer details and do not enter payment information.

Georgia Candy WoZ

Step into a candy store. One partner is the customer; the other plays the worker behind the system. Build a conversation together, then explore what happens when you turn it into intent labels.

Discussion questions

  1. Which turns were hard to label? Could one turn serve more than one purpose, or fit none of the available categories?
  2. What did you use from the surrounding conversation that a classifier looking at one turn might miss?
  3. How did the customer's role and the worker's responses change the conversation? What kinds of examples are missing from our class dataset?
  4. How might fixed intent categories encourage people to change the way they speak? Who might find that difficult, and what would make the interaction more accessible?
  5. What is lost when a conversation becomes rows labelled Human or Worker? How well did the plan for the interaction capture what actually happened?
  6. After the class test, examine a misclassification. Does it suggest a missing example, an unclear category, or a limitation of the approach?
